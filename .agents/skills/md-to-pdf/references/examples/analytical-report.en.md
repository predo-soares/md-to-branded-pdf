# Sample Onboarding Analysis

This synthetic report illustrates a general product analysis and contains no real client data.

## Thesis

A shorter first-run path may help new users understand the product's first task. The report treats this as a hypothesis rather than a proven result.

## Evidence

A fictional team reviewed notes from five sample task sessions. Three participants paused at the same menu label, while two continued without hesitation. These values are illustrative and are not presented as a statistical finding. The [methods reference](https://example.org/methods) describes a possible way to record the sessions.

The team also compared the wording on the start screen with the wording in the help guide. The terms differed. One document used “workspace,” while the other used “project.” The mismatch is visible in the synthetic source material, but the report does not assume that it caused every observed pause.

| Synthetic source | Observation | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Session notes | Three of five participants paused | Small sample |
| Start screen | Uses “workspace” | Wording may have changed |
| Help guide | Uses “project” | May not be read during task |

The table preserves the source of each observation. It does not convert the count into a rate or imply that terminology explains every pause.

## Analysis

The evidence suggests a small terminology test. A revised label could be tested against the existing label in a controlled follow-up. The team would observe whether users choose the correct starting action and how confidently they describe it. A single round would narrow the question; it would not prove a long-term business outcome.

The analysis distinguishes observations, interpretation, and possible action. The observed pause belongs in the evidence section. The interpretation is that terminology may be unclear. The proposed action is to test a clearer label. Keeping these layers separate makes the reasoning easier to review and challenge.

A useful follow-up would compare the current wording with one alternative while keeping the task prompt and starting screen consistent. The team would predefine what counts as a correct first action and record explanations from both successful and unsuccessful attempts. The report would show the raw counts before discussing their meaning.

## Risks

The sample is small and may not represent all users. Participants may also interpret the test scenario differently from a real task. The existing help guide may have changed since the notes were collected. The team should verify these assumptions before treating the proposed label as a final design decision.

- A facilitator could unintentionally guide a participant toward the intended control.
- A participant might pause to read carefully rather than because a label is unclear.
- The product may have changed between the source notes and the next test.

## Conclusion

Run a short terminology test and compare task completion and participant explanations. Record both successful and unsuccessful paths. Report the evidence before making a broader recommendation, and keep the next iteration small enough to review.

If the follow-up does not support the terminology hypothesis, return to the task map and examine other points of friction. The recommendation is conditional on the next set of observations, not a claim that a shorter path will improve a real product.
